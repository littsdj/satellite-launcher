The sky is getting crowded

Every satellite we launch shares the sky with everything launched before it.

The crowded sky in level 3 was a simplified model, but real orbits are busy too. About 40,000 objects are tracked in space, and only about 11,000 of them are working satellites. The rest include dead satellites and pieces of debris.

What is Kessler syndrome?

Picture two satellites crashing into each other. They break into lots of pieces. Those pieces fly around Earth, and any one of them can hit something else.

Each new crash makes more pieces. More pieces mean more chances for crashes. That is a chain reaction.

In 1978, NASA scientists Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais warned that this could happen if enough objects were in orbit. The European Space Agency (ESA) calls it the Kessler syndrome, and says it could make some orbits unusable.

The full runaway chain reaction has not happened. It is a risk, and scientists are working to prevent it.

Why small pieces matter

  • Objects in low Earth orbit travel about 7 to 8 km every second. That is about 28,000 km/h.
  • When two objects collide, they hit each other at about 10 km per second on average.
  • Debris can be as small as a fleck of paint.

A real crash: Iridium 33 and Cosmos 2251

On 10 February 2009, a working US communications satellite, Iridium 33, crashed into a dead Russian satellite, Cosmos 2251. It happened about 790 km above Earth.

It was the first accidental collision between two whole satellites. It made more than 2,000 tracked pieces of debris in total.

Not by accident: in 2007, China deliberately destroyed its own old weather satellite, Fengyun-1C, with a missile. That made more than 2,000 tracked pieces too, in one of the biggest debris clouds ever.

Still up there: Vanguard 1

You met it in levels 2 and 3. Vanguard 1 was launched by the United States on 17 March 1958. It is the oldest human-made satellite still orbiting Earth.

It stopped transmitting in 1964. It went silent, but it is still circling.

Why it matters

We use satellites every day, for:

  • weather forecasts
  • GPS and satnav
  • TV
  • internet and phone calls
  • science

Every object left behind is one more thing a new launch has to avoid. The International Space Station has moved out of the way of space debris more than 40 times since 1999.

What people are doing

  • Watching. The US Space Surveillance Network tracks objects generally larger than about 10 cm.
  • Clearing up sooner. In 2022, the US FCC adopted a rule that satellites in low Earth orbit (below 2,000 km) must be removed no later than 5 years after their mission ends. The older guideline was 25 years.
  • Making less debris. ESA's Zero Debris approach aims to greatly limit new debris by 2030. ESA also cut its own limit from 25 years to 5.
  • Cleaning up. ESA's ClearSpace-1 mission is planned to capture an old ESA satellite, PROBA-1 (launched in 2001), and remove it. It has not launched yet.
